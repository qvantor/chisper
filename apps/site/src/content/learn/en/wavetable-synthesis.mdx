Wavetable synthesis makes a sound by playing one wave out of a table of many, and moving through the table while a
note plays. Every wave in the table is a single cycle of a different shape, so each has a timbre of its own: moving
from one to the next turns a soft sine into a bright saw, one vowel into another, a clean tone into a growl. This
ability to change timbre over time is what makes wavetable synthesis so useful for evolving basses, leads and pads.
This page explains the idea piece by piece, with something to hear at every step; the examples run on Chisper, a free
wavetable synth that plays in the browser.

## Wavetable synthesis in one minute

1. A wavetable is a collection of single-cycle waves, called frames. In Chisper, a table can contain up to 256 frames,
   usually of 2048 samples each.
2. The oscillator plays one frame in a loop. The key you press sets how fast the loop runs, which is the pitch, and
   the shape of the frame sets the timbre.
3. The **Wave** control selects which frame plays.
4. Something moves the position: your hand or, better, an envelope, an LFO or the velocity. The movement is what makes
   a wavetable sound alive.
5. The rest is an ordinary synth. Unison thickens the voices, a filter shapes them, an envelope gives them their
   loudness and effects finish the sound.

## What is a wavetable, and what is in a frame?

Think of one cycle of a wave as a recipe. Repeated over and over it is a tone, and its shape is its timbre. Shape and
harmonics are the same thing seen two ways: every repeating wave is a sum of sine waves at whole multiples of its
pitch, the harmonics, and the shape says how loud each of them is.

- A sine is the plain case, the first harmonic and nothing else: a pure, soft tone, like a tuning fork.
- A saw wave contains all integer harmonics, with each harmonic decreasing in amplitude inversely with its frequency:
  the second is half as loud, the third a third as loud. It sounds bright and buzzy, the base of strings and brass.
- Keep only the odd harmonics, the 1st, 3rd, 5th and so on, and you hear the hollow square of a clarinet.
- Narrow the pulse of a square and more energy moves into the upper harmonics. The tone gets thin and nasal.

A wavetable lines up many such cycles, one recipe after another. A frame may be a basic shape, one cycle cut from a
recorded voice or instrument, or a wave drawn by hand, and the table keeps them in order, so that walking along it
changes the harmonics a little at a time.

## How does a wavetable oscillator make a note?

It reads the frame round and round. For an A at 440 Hz the oscillator goes through the cycle 440 times a second,
whether the frame is 2048 samples long or 600. Pitch and timbre stay apart: the key decides how often the cycle
repeats, the frame decides what each repetition looks like.

## What does the Wave knob do?

**Wave** is the position in the table. Its travel is divided into equal parts, one for every frame, and you hear the
frame whose part the knob is in. The strip under the wave shows where you are (*Wave 33/256*).

What happens between two frames depends on the table. With few frames the sound moves in steps: *Basic Shapes*, the
table of *Init*, has nine, a saw, a sine, a triangle, two squares and four narrow pulses (25, 12.5, 6.25 and 3.125%),
and turning **Wave** makes the tone jump from one to the next. Give the same turn a table of 256 frames and it becomes
a glide, because every step is only 1/256 of the knob.

A jump from one frame to the next is clean, with no click. Some tables with few frames are morph tables: Chisper fills
the gaps between their frames, so they glide like a table of 256. Every table made from an audio file is a morph
table.

<Tip>

A table that sounds steppy as you turn **Wave** has few frames. Pick one with more, or make a morph table from audio
(see [Wavetables: import, make from audio, draw](/manual/wavetables/)).

</Tip>

## How do you make a wavetable move?

Wavetable synthesis lives in the movement: a table left on one frame is only an ordinary oscillator. **Wave** is a
knob like any other, so any source of modulation can drive it. Drag the tab of an envelope, an LFO or VELO onto the
knob and it moves (the whole mechanism is on [Modulation: envelopes, LFOs, macros and
velocity](/manual/modulation-envelopes-lfo/)).

An envelope turns the knob once for every note. A short decay across the table is the snap of a pluck, a slow attack
a swell. An LFO never stops, so the tone drifts back and forth through the table: a pad that breathes, a lead that
shimmers, and once the LFO follows the tempo the drift turns into rhythm. Velocity brings in the player: the harder
you press a key, the further into the table the note starts.

Two oscillators mean two positions. One can rise while the other falls, or each gets its own table and its own speed,
creating more complex and evolving textures. The presets *UFO Drone* and *Biting Brass Keys* work like this: the first
sweeps the wavetable position through two tables with envelopes, the second moves the position of both oscillators with
LFO 1.

## What else shapes a wavetable sound?

A wavetable is only the source. The chain after it is familiar from any synth.

**Unison** stacks up to 8 copies of the note in each oscillator. **Detune** spreads them in pitch, **Blend** balances
the centre voices against the side ones (75% by default), and **Phase** and **Phase rand** decide where the voices
start. A bright saw table in an 8-voice unison is the supersaw. **Oct**, **Semi** and **Fine** tune each oscillator,
which lets two tables an octave or a fifth apart act as one sound, and the **Sub** oscillator and the **Noise** player
add a low tone or a sample (white noise, a hat, wind) underneath.

Then comes the filter, one for every voice. A wavetable hands it a source that already changes, so it often has less
to do than on a plain saw. Distortion, chorus, delay, a compressor, a reverb and an EQ finish the sound, in any
order.

## Wavetable, subtractive, FM, additive and sampling: what is the difference?

| Method | What makes the tone | How the tone changes | Known for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subtractive | A few fixed waves (saw, square) | A filter takes harmonics away | Classic analog basses and leads |
| Wavetable | A table of many different waves | A position moves through the table | Evolving pads, growls, digital and vocal tones |
| FM | Sine waves that modulate each other's pitch | The amount of the modulation | Bells, electric pianos, metallic tones |
| Additive | Many sines added one by one | The level of every harmonic | Organs, spectral textures |
| Sampling | A recording | The pitch, the loop and the filter | Real instruments |

The methods mix. Most wavetable synths put a filter after the oscillators, so they are subtractive as well: the table
is a source that moves, the filter carves it. A frame is also a list of harmonics, which makes wavetable a cousin of
additive synthesis, and a table cut from an audio file is a cousin of sampling.

3. Play a few notes, holding each for about three seconds.
4. Raise **Decay** of ENV 2 to about 2 s and play them again the same way.

## Questions people ask

### Is wavetable synthesis the same as sampling?

No. A sampler plays back one recording of a sound; a wavetable synth plays many one-cycle waves and moves between
them. The two do meet in one trick: cut a wavetable from an audio file and its frames become short slices of that
sound.

### Do I need wavetables of my own?

No. Chisper comes with a library of wavetables sorted into folders, and each one downloads when you pick it. Make your
own for a sound the library lacks: draw a wave, import a WAV wavetable or turn an audio file into one.

### Why does my wavetable sound steppy?

Because the table has few frames and **Wave** hops from one to the next. A table of 256 frames, or a morph table,
glides instead.

### Can a wavetable hold a real instrument?

Yes. A frame can be one cycle of a recorded note, and a table can be a row of such cycles from the start of the note
to its end. *Harp Pluck* plays tables of real instruments, a harp and a nylon guitar an octave above.
